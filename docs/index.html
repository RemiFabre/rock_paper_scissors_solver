Generalised rock paper scissors

The Strongest Option Trap

When one option beats more things than the others, you should not play it more often. Here is why, with a solver you can poke at and a reader you can try to beat.

1. The game

Two players pick simultaneously. The attacker picks a row, the defender picks a column, and the cell says who wins: +1 the attacker, -1 the defender, 0 nobody. Plain rock paper scissors is the Balanced preset below. In the Unbalanced preset, A1 also beats D2: it wins against two of the three defences, while every other option wins against one.

Edit the payoff matrix

Optimal play

Exact solution by enumerating square sub-matrices (Shapley and Snow, 1950), the same method as rps_solver.py. Optimal means: the mix whose worst case is best. Against it, the opponent gains nothing by knowing it.

Rock paper scissors solves to a third each, with value zero. The unbalanced game solves to A0 4/9, A1 3/9, A2 2/9. The option that wins against two defences is played less than the option that wins against one. The attacker still comes out ahead: the game is worth +1/9 per round to them.

2. Why you cannot just play the strong option

The defender is not a coin. If A1 shows up half the time, D0 (the only defence that beats it) becomes the obvious answer, and the attacker's habit turns into the defender's income. 3. Indifference: the optimum is where the opponent stops caring

Look at the bars when the point sits on the optimum: the three are level. That is not a coincidence, it is the definition. If one defence earned less than the others, the defender would never pick it, and the attacker could shift weight to punish the defences that remain. The attack mix is chosen so that the defender has nothing to choose. Solving those equalities is all the solver does:

attacker's payoff if the defender plays D0: A0 - A1
attacker's payoff if the defender plays D1: A1 - A2
attacker's payoff if the defender plays D2: -A0 + A1 + A2
set all three equal, add A0 + A1 + A2 = 1 → A = [4/9, 3/9, 2/9], each line worth 1/9

Read the first two lines again. A1 is the option D0 wants to see and D1 fears. Its frequency is set by the balance between those two defences, and by nothing else. In particular, it is not set by how strong A1 is against D2.

Your frequencies are decided by the opponent's incentives, not by your own options' strength. The strong option's job is to make its counter worth playing; the counter's counter is what you end up playing most.

4. Buffing an option does not make it played more

Slide the payoff of A1 against D2 from 0 (a neutral trade) to 3 (a crushing win). The optimal frequency of A1 stays at exactly one third. The buff flows elsewhere: the defender leans harder on D0 to keep A1 in check, so the attacker plays more A0, the option that beats D0. At 3, A2 is not needed at all.

5. Try to beat a reader

Play the attacker with the buttons. The defender does nothing clever: it watches the last few moves you made, predicts the next one from those patterns, and plays the counter. Most people are down within forty rounds. Then hand over to the optimal mix and watch the score drift up at 1/9 per round, whatever the reader tries.

The reader is a small context model over your last three moves (with fallbacks to shorter contexts), best-replying to its prediction. It exploits any regularity. Against a truly random optimal mix its prediction is worthless.

6. What "optimal" does and does not mean

The optimal mix is a floor. It guarantees the game's value no matter who is on the other side, and it cannot be read because there is nothing to read. But it is not a ceiling. In the balanced game its value is zero: it never loses, and never wins. Against an opponent who plays badly it does not win more than 1/9 per round either, because it was designed against the best possible defender, not this one.

Beating people rather than drawing with them requires a second layer: model the opponent, best-reply to the model, and bound how far from the floor you are willing to stand. The companion documents in the repository cover that layer (safe exploitation, and how many rounds it takes to prove an edge), and a way to apply all of this to fighting games, where each encounter is a small matrix like the ones above and the entries depend on the situation.
